# Content Guide

## 1. Purpose

MindSpark content should help teenagers practice thinking, not memorize facts. Every lesson must introduce a thinker, explain one powerful idea, connect it to a modern teenage situation, and ask the student to apply it.

The content should feel smart, clear, and alive. It should never feel like a school textbook, a moral lecture, or a quote collection.

## 2. Audience

Primary audience: students aged 15-18.

Assume the reader:

- Can handle complex ideas when explained clearly.
- Dislikes being talked down to.
- Responds to stories, dilemmas, identity, fairness, ambition, friendship, fear, technology, and purpose.
- Needs abstract ideas translated into everyday situations.
- Benefits from intellectual tension instead of simple moral answers.

## 3. Editorial Promise

Each lesson should answer this product promise:

> Here is a human who saw the world differently. Here is the problem they faced. Here is the idea they used. Now try using that idea in your world.

## 4. Voice and Tone

The voice should be:

- Intelligent.
- Clear.
- Slightly provocative.
- Respectful.
- Emotionally relevant.
- Concrete.
- Curious.

The voice should not be:

- Childish.
- Preachy.
- Academic for its own sake.
- Overly inspirational.
- Politically simplistic.
- Quote-first.
- Exam-focused.

Bad:

> Today we shall learn about Socrates, a famous Greek philosopher, and his contribution to philosophy.

Better:

> Socrates became dangerous because he asked people why they believed what they believed. Powerful people did not enjoy being questioned in public.

Bad:

> Always tell the truth because Gandhi said truth is important.

Better:

> Gandhi believed truth was not just something you said. It was something you had to live, especially when lying would make life easier.

## 5. Lesson Structure

Every lesson must include 8 blocks.

| Block | Goal | User action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hook | Create curiosity | Tap to continue |
| Human Story | Make the thinker real | Read or listen |
| Big Idea | Explain one idea clearly | Understand |
| Thinking Tool | Give a reusable mental method | Apply |
| Modern Test | Place the idea in a teen scenario | Choose, rank, or decide |
| Reflection Challenge | Make the idea personal | Write or speak |
| Thought Tension | Show a counter-view | Respond |
| Reward | Reinforce growth | Unlock skill, XP, badge, quote |

## 6. The 7 Quality Gates

A lesson is not ready unless it answers all 7 questions:

1. Who was this person?
2. What problem did they care about?
3. What was their big idea?
4. Why was the idea dangerous, unusual, or powerful?
5. How does it apply to a teenager today?
6. What question should the student ask themselves?
7. What action or challenge should they do?

Use this as the final editorial checklist before shipping content.

## 8. Block-Level Guidance

### Hook

The hook should create tension or curiosity in one sentence.

Good hook patterns:

- "Socrates was killed for asking too many questions."
- "Ambedkar asked why society called unfair rules normal."
- "Marie Curie chased invisible energy before the world understood its danger."
- "Einstein trusted imagination before calculation."

Avoid:

- Birth dates.
- Long introductions.
- Generic praise.
- "One of the greatest..." openings.

### Human Story

The story should make the thinker feel human. Include:

- What world they lived in.
- What problem they noticed.
- What they questioned.
- What made their view unusual.
- What it cost them or demanded from them.

Keep it short. For the Full Lesson layer, aim for 120-180 words. For Quick Spark, aim for 45-70 words.

### Big Idea

The Big Idea should be one clear idea, not a summary of the thinker's entire philosophy.

Bad:

> Kant transformed epistemology, metaphysics, and moral philosophy.

Better:

> Kant believed doing the right thing matters most when it is difficult, not when it is convenient.

### Thinking Tool

The Thinking Tool turns the idea into a method the student can use.

Examples:

| Thinker | Tool |
| --- | --- |
| Socrates | Ask "why?" three times before accepting an opinion |
| Buddha | Notice desire before reacting |
| Einstein | Try a thought experiment before looking for an answer |
| Ambedkar | Ask who benefits from the system |
| Tagore | Look for what makes a life fully human, not only useful |
| Mandela | Separate justice from revenge |

### Modern Test

The Modern Test should use teenage situations. It should not be a history quiz.

Good topics:

- Social media pressure.
- Online bullying.
- AI and homework.
- Exam competition.
- Friendship conflict.
- Family expectations.
- Climate anxiety.
- Body image.
- Career pressure.
- Money and success.
- Religion and identity.
- Technology addiction.
- Cancel culture.

The best scenarios have no easy answer.

### Reflection Challenge

The reflection prompt should ask the student to apply the idea personally.

Good:

> Write one belief you have that you may have never questioned properly.

Good:

> Describe one rule in your school or family that looks fair on the surface but may not be fair underneath.

Avoid:

- "Do you agree? Explain."
- "Write what you learned."
- Prompts that ask for a summary instead of self-examination.

### Thought Tension

Thought Tension prevents the app from preaching. It introduces a serious counter-view.

Example:

> But if everyone questions every rule all the time, can a society function?

The student should be asked to respond, revise, or defend their view.

### Reward

Rewards should connect to mental growth.

Example:

> Skill unlocked: Assumption Testing.
> Badge earned: The Questioner.

Avoid rewards that feel random or unrelated to the idea.

## 9. Layered Content Rules

Each lesson can have multiple depth layers.

### Quick Spark

Goal: One idea in 60 seconds.

Include:

- Hook.
- Short story.
- Big idea.
- One question.

### Full Lesson

Goal: Core experience in 3-5 minutes.

Include all 8 lesson blocks.

### Deep Dive

Goal: Post-MVP serious exploration.

May include:

- Timeline.
- Opposing thinker.
- Historical context.
- Primary source excerpt.
- Related modern debate.

Deep Dive is reserved for later. Do not let it slow MVP content.

## 10. Modern Scenario Rules

A scenario should be:

- Specific.
- Familiar to teenagers.
- Emotionally realistic.
- Open to more than one view.
- Connected to the thinker's idea.

Bad:

> What is justice?

Better:

> Your school says everyone has equal opportunity. Some students have tutors, laptops, quiet rooms, and private coaching. Others do not. Is the competition still fair?

Bad:

> Should people be brave?

Better:

> Your friends are mocking someone online. You know it is cruel, but speaking up may make you the next target. What would courage look like here?

## 11. Quiz and Choice Design

Choices should test reasoning, not trivia.

Use:

- Dilemma choices.
- Ranking values.
- "Which question would this thinker ask first?"
- "Which argument is strongest?"
- "What assumption is hidden here?"

Avoid:

- Birth year questions.
- One-word definitions.
- Gotcha questions.
- Shaming wrong answers.

When there is a best answer, explain why. When multiple answers are defensible, say so and show the tradeoff.

## 12. Sensitive Topic Guidelines

Topics such as caste, race, religion, gender, violence, colonialism, sexuality, politics, and oppression require extra care.

Rules:

- Do not flatten historical suffering into a simple life lesson.
- Do not present oppressed groups as props for inspiration.
- Avoid stereotypes and caricatures.
- Use precise historical language.
- Explain context without overwhelming the lesson.
- Include Thought Tension when the topic has serious disagreement.
- Do not ask students to disclose traumatic personal experiences.
- Prefer reflection prompts that allow distance, such as observing a system, rule, or idea.

Editorial review should be required before publishing sensitive lessons.

## 13. Quote Card Rules

Quote cards must never be standalone quote posters.

Every quote card must include:

- Quote.
- Thinker.
- Plain meaning.
- Historical or intellectual context.
- What this means today.
- Reflection question.

Example:

```text
Quote:
"The unexamined life is not worth living." - Socrates

Plain meaning:
A life where you never question your beliefs, actions, or values is not fully your own.

Context:
Socrates said this while defending his life of questioning in Athens.

Today:
Social media, friends, and family can quietly hand us beliefs. Socrates asks whether we have tested them.

Question:
Which part of your life are you running on autopilot?
```

## 14. Skill Mapping

Every lesson should map to one primary skill and up to two secondary skills.

MVP skills:

- Questioning.
- Logic.
- Empathy.
- Imagination.
- Courage.
- Discipline.
- Justice.
- Creativity.
- Self-awareness.
- Systems thinking.

Examples:

| Thinker | Primary skill | Possible secondary skills |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Socrates | Questioning | Logic, Courage |
| Buddha | Self-awareness | Empathy, Discipline |
| Ambedkar | Justice | Systems thinking, Courage |
| Einstein | Imagination | Curiosity, Logic |
| Curie | Discipline | Courage, Scientific skepticism |
| Tagore | Creativity | Empathy, Freedom |

If a skill is not in the MVP list, either map it to the closest existing skill or propose adding it during product review.

## 16. Content Review Checklist

Before publishing a lesson, confirm:

- The lesson has all 8 required blocks.
- The 7 quality gates are answered.
- The lesson has one central idea, not many.
- The hook creates curiosity.
- The story shows the thinker as human.
- The modern scenario feels relevant to ages 15-18.
- The reflection prompt asks for real thinking.
- Thought Tension presents a serious counter-view.
- The reward maps to a relevant skill.
- No answer shames the student.
- Sensitive topics are handled carefully.
- Language is clear and not academic.
- Reading time fits the chosen layer.
- The lesson has been checked for factual accuracy.

## 17. Example Lesson Draft

Thinker: B. R. Ambedkar

Lesson title: Fair Rules Are Not Always Fair Systems

Primary skill: Systems thinking

Secondary skill: Justice

Hook:

> Ambedkar did not only ask for better treatment. He asked why society was designed to deny dignity to some people.

Human Story:

> B. R. Ambedkar grew up in a society where caste decided how people were treated before they had a chance to prove who they were. He became a scholar, lawyer, and one of the chief architects of India's Constitution, but his deepest question was not only legal. It was moral: can a society call itself fair if its rules protect dignity for some people and humiliation for others?

Big Idea:

> A society is not fair just because it has rules. It is fair only when those rules protect everyone's dignity.

Thinking Tool:

> Ask who benefits from the system and who pays the hidden cost.

Modern Test:

> Your school says everyone has equal opportunity. Some students can afford tutors, laptops, quiet rooms, and private coaching. Others cannot. Is the competition still fair?

Reflection Challenge:

> Identify one system in your life that looks fair on the surface but may not be fair underneath.

Thought Tension:

> But if everyone takes the same exam under the same rules, is that not fairness? Explain where you agree or disagree.

Reward:

> Skill unlocked: Systems Thinking. Badge earned: Justice Seeker.
